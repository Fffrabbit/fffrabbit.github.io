# The Connection Between Smartphone-Related Psychological Phenomena, Cognitive Functions, and Self-Regulation Among IT Students in Higher Education

My master's thesis examines whether smartphone-related psychological phenomena — brain drain, nomophobia, fear of missing out (FoMO), and problematic phone use — are linked to IT students' cognitive load, academic self-regulation, and attention disruption. The thesis was completed in May 2026 at the University of Jyväskylä's Faculty of Information Technology, in the educational technology programme, supervised by Ville Isomöttönen.

## Method

The study was a quantitative cross-sectional survey. Data was collected via an electronic questionnaire in spring 2026, with 79 IT higher-education students responding — the sample grew from roughly 48 to a final 79 during data collection. The phenomena were measured with validated instruments: the NASA-TLX workload index, the SAS phone addiction scale, and the FoMO and nomophobia questionnaires (NMP-Q, FoMOS), with academic self-regulation measured via MSLQ subscales. Data was analysed in Jamovi using correlation and factor analysis and linear regression.

The target group changed partway through the process: the original plan was to study 9th-grade pupils in Hämeenlinna, but due to research-permission issues the target group shifted to IT higher-education students.

## Key findings

All four phenomena studied correlated significantly with one another. The strongest link was between problematic phone use and attention disruption (ρ = 0.85, p < .001) — strong enough that the two phenomena appear nearly indistinguishable in respondents' experience.

The thesis's central finding concerns *how* the phone affects cognitive load. The results point to two separate pathways:

- **Active, conscious phone use** during study directly predicted cognitive load.
- **The phone's mere passive presence** (e.g. lying on the desk) did not directly predict cognitive load, but strongly predicted nomophobia and problematic use.

This refines Ward et al.'s (2017) brain drain theory: a device's passive presence doesn't appear to load cognition directly, but instead sustains background nomophobic anxiety, which in turn exposes the student to active study interruptions — and it's those interruptions that explain the actual rise in cognitive load.

In age and study-stage comparisons, the youngest students (18–23) and bachelor's-stage students reported significantly more FoMO and nomophobia than older or master's-stage students. Heavy social-media users also reported weaker self-regulation skills than moderate users.

## Conclusion

Based on the results, blanket bans are not an effective solution in higher education, since they don't target students' actual self-regulation difficulties. Instead, the thesis recommends investing in students' self-regulation skills, digital literacy, and digital hygiene — for example, identifying personal distraction triggers and building conscious digital routines rather than imposing outright bans.

## Limitations

The thesis names its own key limitations: the cross-sectional design doesn't allow causal claims, the data relies on self-report, and the sample size (N=79) is smaller than recommended for multivariate methods, so the results should be read as indicative rather than conclusive. The attention measure is also conceptually tied specifically to phone-related disruption rather than general attentional capacity.

## Theoretical background and instruments

Core frameworks: Brain Drain theory (Ward et al. 2017), Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller 1994), FoMO (Przybylski et al. 2013), and Nomophobia (Yildirim & Correia 2015). Instruments used: NMP-Q, FoMOS, SAS-SV, MSLQ, and NASA-TLX.
