# AI in Higher Education – A Comparison and Practical Guide

Finnish higher education institutions have each written their own policies on using AI in teaching and studying, but the policies differ in content. On this page I've put together a comparison of the institutions where I'd be willing to work (the Helsinki region, Tampere, Kanta-Häme, Päijät-Häme and Central Finland), along with practical guidance on what the differences mean for everyday student life.

## Why this comparison

Universities' AI policies have already been studied academically: in a 2025 article, Maunula, Maunumäki and Lähdesmäki compared the policies of nine Finnish universities against the EU's ethical AI requirements, and found that the policies emphasise responsibility but give less attention to ethical and pedagogical guidance ([read the article](https://lehti.yliopistopedagogiikka.fi/2025/06/04/analyysi-suomalaisten-yliopistojen-tekoalyohjeistuksien-eettisyydesta/)). This page isn't meant to repeat that study, but to complement it in two ways: by also covering universities of applied sciences, and by taking a more practical angle, namely what a student can actually do based on the guidelines.

## Shared background: Arene's recommendations

All universities of applied sciences follow the national recommendation and so-called traffic light model published by Arene (the Rectors' Conference of Finnish Universities of Applied Sciences), originally from 12 June 2023 and updated 4/2026, on which each institution bases its own refined version. The policies also openly build on one another: JAMK states in its own guide that it drew on Satakunta University of Applied Sciences' (Samk) model with permission. The model itself is simple:

- 🟢 **Green**: AI may be used to support the task, no separate reporting needed.
- 🟡 **Yellow**: AI may be used, but the use must be briefly described: at what stage AI was used, which tool, and how its output was applied.
- 🔴 **Red**: AI may not be used to complete the task.

The teacher indicates which colour applies for each assignment. Haaga-Helia uses a comparable icon system per assignment.

## What the differences mean for a student

The details vary, but a few principles hold in practice almost everywhere:

- AI use is generally allowed as a learning aid unless the course teacher has restricted it. Responsibility for the final content always rests with the student.
- A course-specific instruction always overrides the general policy. Check what's allowed for the specific course or assignment first.
- Presenting AI-generated text as one's own without disclosure counts as plagiarism in every policy I found, regardless of whether it's a university or a university of applied sciences.
- In theses and dissertations, AI can generally be used for ideation, structuring and language editing, but not as a scientific source or as a substitute for one's own thinking. LAB even provides ready-made text templates that can be copied straight into a thesis's reporting section.
- If your own institution provides an official AI tool (for example Aalto AI Assistant, the University of Helsinki's CurreChat, or an institution's own version of Copilot), using it is generally preferable from a security standpoint over third-party services, since data entered there isn't used to train the underlying models.
- Every AI-related policy I found asks students to keep their conversations or prompts, so that use can be verified afterwards if needed.

## Principles for ethical use

The research mentioned earlier found that accessibility and equity are often given less attention in higher-education AI policies. This comparison, though, turned up two interesting exceptions worth highlighting as good practice:

- **LAB has published its guidelines in plain Finnish (selkosuomi) as well** — a concrete accessibility measure, not just a stated principle.
- **Haaga-Helia raises AI's energy consumption** ("traditional search engines use less energy") — an ecological angle not found in any other policy.
- **Laurea forbids recording AI tools by default** (e.g. Teams or Zoom meeting bots) without prior consent from all participants — a practical privacy detail that also applies to group work and interviews.

Beyond these, a few general principles worth keeping in mind regardless of how a specific policy is worded:

- **Equity**: if a course requires AI use, the tool in question should be freely available to everyone. Requiring a paid tool puts students in an unequal position.
- **Data protection**: don't feed AI tools personal data, exam answers, or other confidential material, unless it's your institution's own, security-vetted service.
- **Copyright**: don't feed AI tools copyrighted material, such as textbook text, without permission.
- **Critical thinking**: AI-generated content can be inaccurate or misleading, and it can also invent sources that don't exist. Responsibility for accuracy always rests with whoever presents the content.
- **Transparency**: when it's unclear whether use should be reported, report it anyway, even if the policy doesn't explicitly require it.
